# What is the Carrera Unified Formulation?

The mathematical idea underlying the Carrera Unified Formulation is not specific to structural mechanics. It begins with the general possibility of representing, or approximating, a function through an expansion in a chosen set of basis functions.

Consider a scalar function $f(y,z)$ defined over a two-dimensional domain. Given a suitable family of functions $F_\tau(y,z)$, one may formally write

$$f(y,z)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}F_\tau(y,z)a_\tau$$

where the coefficients $a_\tau$ determine the contribution of each basis function. Depending on the mathematical setting, the functions $F_\tau$ may be monomials, orthogonal polynomials, trigonometric functions, or other suitable basis functions.

In practical calculations, the infinite expansion is replaced by a finite one:

$$f(y,z)\simeq\sum_{\tau=1}^{M}F_\tau(y,z)a_\tau$$

This truncation defines a finite-dimensional approximation space. The function can reproduce only the distributions generated by the selected functions $F_1,\ldots,F_M$. Increasing $M$ enlarges this space and permits progressively richer distributions to be represented.

The Carrera Unified Formulation applies this general mathematical idea to the displacement field of a structural model.

Consider a beam whose longitudinal coordinate is $x$ and whose cross-section is described by $y$ and $z$. For every fixed value of $x$, each displacement component is a function defined over the cross-section. The displacement field can therefore be expanded with respect to the transverse variables $y$ and $z$.

Applying the expansion to the three displacement components gives the generic CUF representation

$$\mathbf{u}(x,y,z)\simeq\sum_{\tau=1}^{M}F_\tau(y,z)\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$$

The coefficients are now vector functions of $x$, because the expansion is performed only over the cross-sectional coordinates. At every longitudinal position, the functions $\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$ determine how much each transverse basis function contributes to the displacement field.

This equation should not be interpreted simply as an alternative way of writing the displacement field. It is a finite-dimensional approximation of its dependence on the cross-sectional coordinates.

For a Maclaurin-type polynomial expansion, the transverse basis may contain

$$1,\quad y,\quad z,\quad y^2,\quad yz,\quad z^2,\quad\ldots$$

A scalar displacement component can then be written as

$$u(x,y,z)\simeq u_1(x)+yu_2(x)+zu_3(x)+y^2u_4(x)+yzu_5(x)+z^2u_6(x)+\cdots$$

The functions $u_1(x),u_2(x),\ldots$ are the longitudinal coefficients of one displacement field. They are not separate physical displacement fields.

At this point, CUF introduces its central modeling principle: the selected transverse expansion defines the admissible cross-sectional kinematics. A low-order expansion permits only a restricted family of deformation patterns. A higher-order expansion enlarges the approximation space and allows warping and other complex cross-sectional deformation modes to emerge.

The structural theory is therefore determined by the selected approximation space. CUF does not need to begin with a predefined beam theory. Classical and higher-order theories can instead be generated systematically by changing the functions $F_\tau(y,z)$, their number, or the expansion order.

## From the displacement expansion to the structural equations

A beam is a three-dimensional body in which one spatial direction is predominant. Let $x$ denote the longitudinal coordinate, while $y$ and $z$ describe the cross-section.

The beam occupies the domain

$$\mathcal{B}=[0,L]\times\Omega$$

where $L$ is the beam length and $\Omega$ is the cross-sectional domain.

In this initial formulation, $\Omega$ is independent of $x$. The same cross-section is therefore assumed along the entire beam axis. The beam is prismatic, although no particular cross-sectional shape is prescribed.

The unknown physical quantity is the three-dimensional displacement vector

$$\mathbf{u}(x,y,z)=\left(u_x(x,y,z),u_y(x,y,z),u_z(x,y,z)\right)^T$$

For every fixed value of $x$, the displacement components are functions defined over the cross-section $\Omega$. Their dependence on the transverse coordinates can therefore be approximated through a selected family of functions $F_\tau(y,z)$.

CUF writes the displacement field as

$$\mathbf{u}(x,y,z)\simeq\sum_{\tau=1}^{M}F_\tau(y,z)\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$$

The functions $F_\tau(y,z)$ describe the admissible variation of the displacement field over the cross-section. The corresponding vector functions

$$\mathbf{u}*\tau(x)=\left(u*{x\tau}(x),u_{y\tau}(x),u_{z\tau}(x)\right)^T$$

describe how the contribution of each transverse function varies along the beam axis.

The functions $\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$ are unknown. They are not calculated by expanding an already known displacement field. They are the generalized displacement functions that must be determined by solving the structural problem.

For a complete two-dimensional polynomial expansion of order $N$, the transverse basis contains the monomials

$$F_\tau(y,z)=y^iz^j$$

whose exponents satisfy

$$i+j\leq N$$

The number of transverse functions is

$$M=\frac{(N+1)(N+2)}{2}$$

The first approximation spaces are

$$N=0:\qquad 1$$

$$N=1:\qquad 1,\quad y,\quad z$$

$$N=2:\qquad 1,\quad y,\quad z,\quad y^2,\quad yz,\quad z^2$$

Each order contains all the functions belonging to the preceding orders. Increasing $N$ therefore enlarges the kinematic approximation space without changing the general formulation.

Once the displacement expansion has been introduced, its spatial derivatives can be calculated.

Since $F_\tau$ depends only on the transverse coordinates, derivatives with respect to $y$ and $z$ act on the transverse functions:

$$\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial y}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{M}\frac{\partial F_\tau}{\partial y}\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$$

$$\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial z}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{M}\frac{\partial F_\tau}{\partial z}\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$$

The derivative with respect to the longitudinal coordinate acts on the generalized displacement functions:

$$\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial x}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{M}F_\tau(y,z)\frac{d\mathbf{u}_\tau}{dx}$$

This separation between transverse functions and longitudinal unknowns is a central feature of CUF.

Under the assumptions of small displacements and small strains, the strain vector is obtained from the spatial derivatives of the displacement field:

$$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}$$

The operator $\mathbf{D}$ contains the derivatives required by the three-dimensional strain-displacement relations.

For a linear elastic material, the stresses follow from the constitutive law

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$

where $\mathbf{C}$ is the material stiffness matrix.

CUF does not replace the strain-displacement relations or the constitutive law. It supplies the kinematic representation that is substituted into these standard equations of continuum mechanics.

The generalized displacement functions $\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$ are determined by imposing equilibrium. In CUF, the governing equations are commonly derived through the Principle of Virtual Displacements:

$$\delta W_{\mathrm{int}}=\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}}$$

The internal virtual work is evaluated over the complete beam volume:

$$\delta W_{\mathrm{int}}=\int_0^L\int_\Omega\delta\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma},d\Omega,dx$$

After substituting the CUF expansion, the cross-sectional integration produces coefficients that couple every pair of transverse functions $F_\tau$ and $F_s$. The resulting equations govern the longitudinal functions $\mathbf{u}_\tau(x)$.

The logical sequence is therefore

$$\text{displacement expansion}\rightarrow\text{spatial derivatives}\rightarrow\text{strains}\rightarrow\text{stresses}\rightarrow\text{virtual work}\rightarrow\text{governing equations}$$

The approximation order determines the richness of the admissible displacement field, while the governing mechanical equations determine the corresponding generalized displacements.

Classical beam theories can be interpreted as restricted choices within this framework. Higher-order theories retain additional transverse terms and can represent more complex effects such as cross-sectional deformation, warping, Poisson coupling, and nonuniform shear distributions.

The same formulation remains valid when the polynomial order or the family of transverse functions is changed. This is the unifying principle of CUF.

The assumption that $\Omega$ is independent of $x$ will provide the reference point for introducing a cross-section that evolves continuously along the beam axis.
