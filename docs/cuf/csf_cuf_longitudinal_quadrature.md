# DRAFT

>The following material presents the current conceptual definition of the CSF-CUF sectional interface. The coupling is still under development and is intended to be implemented through a dedicated software bridge able to provide the sectional data required by the CUF formulation from the CSF representation $\mathcal{S}(x)$.

# Formal CSF-CUF coupling for a continuous longitudinal section model

# Longitudinal Quadrature for the CSF-CUF Variable-Section Extension

## 1. Scope

In the classical prismatic CUF formulation, the cross-sectional domain is
independent of the longitudinal coordinate.

In the CSF-CUF extension, both the cross-sectional geometry and the
constitutive properties may vary continuously along the beam axis.

This introduces additional longitudinal polynomial dependence into the
sectional coefficients entering the CUF fundamental nucleus.

Consequently, the longitudinal Gauss-Legendre quadrature order cannot be
determined from the longitudinal finite-element interpolation or from the CUF
expansion order alone.

The polynomial degree required for longitudinal quadrature is a property of
the complete integrand and must account for every longitudinally varying
factor entering its algebraic construction.


## 2. Variable CSF sectional coefficient

The sectional coefficients entering the CUF fundamental nucleus have the form

$$J_{\tau s}^{mn}(x) = \sum_k \int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{CSF},k}(x)} C_k^{mn}(x,y,z) \, D_\phi F_\tau(y,z) \, D_\xi F_s(y,z) \, d\Omega$$

where $\Omega_{\mathrm{CSF},k}(x)$ is the $k$-th CSF cross-sectional domain,
$C_k^{mn}$ is the corresponding constitutive coefficient, and
$D_\phi F_\tau$ and $D_\xi F_s$ are transverse CUF expansion functions or
their derivatives.

The dependence of $J_{\tau s}^{mn}(x)$ on the longitudinal coordinate is
therefore generated by several distinct contributions.

In particular, a variable cross-section produces two different geometric
effects:

1. the transverse polynomial is evaluated on coordinates that themselves
   vary along $x$;

2. the cross-sectional differential measure $d\Omega$ varies along $x$.

These two effects are distinct and must be accounted for separately.


## 3. Longitudinal transfer of the transverse polynomial degree

Consider a fixed reference cross-section $\widehat{\Omega}$ with local
coordinates $(\eta,\zeta)$.

The physical transverse coordinates are described by a longitudinally varying
geometric mapping

$$y = y(x,\eta,\zeta)$$

$$z = z(x,\eta,\zeta)$$

Let $p_g$ denote the maximum polynomial degree in $x$ of this transverse
geometric mapping.

For a constant cross-section,

$$p_g = 0$$

because $y$ and $z$ do not depend on $x$.

For an affine variation of the transverse coordinates,

$$p_g = 1$$

and for a more general polynomial geometric law the value of $p_g$ must be
defined by that law.

Now consider a transverse polynomial term

$$T(y,z) = y^i z^j$$

with total transverse degree

$$p_T = i+j$$

When the cross-section varies along the beam axis, the transverse polynomial
becomes

$$T(x,\eta,\zeta) =
y(x,\eta,\zeta)^i
z(x,\eta,\zeta)^j$$

and therefore acquires a longitudinal polynomial dependence.

A conservative upper bound for the induced longitudinal degree is

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} = p_g \, p_T$$

This relation expresses the coupling between the transverse polynomial order
and the longitudinal variation of the cross-sectional geometry.

For a constant section,

$$p_g = 0$$

and therefore

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} = 0$$

regardless of the transverse polynomial degree.

For an affine variable section,

$$p_g = 1$$

and therefore

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} = p_T$$

For example, a transverse polynomial of degree three produces no longitudinal
polynomial contribution in a prismatic section, whereas under an affine
geometric variation it may produce a longitudinal contribution of degree
three.

More generally, if the transverse geometry itself followed a polynomial law
of degree two,

$$p_g = 2$$

the same transverse polynomial of degree three could induce a longitudinal
degree

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} = 2 \times 3 = 6$$

This geometric-transfer effect does not exist in the classical prismatic CUF
case and is therefore specific to the variable-section extension.


## 4. Application to CUF transverse expansions

For a complete total-degree Maclaurin expansion of order $N$,

$$F_\tau(y,z) = y^i z^j$$

with

$$i+j \leq N$$

A product of two transverse expansion functions can therefore reach total
degree

$$p_T \leq 2N$$

before considering transverse derivatives.

Consequently, for a variable geometry of longitudinal degree $p_g$,

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} \leq 2N p_g$$

For the currently considered affine CSF geometric variation,

$$p_g = 1$$

and therefore

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} \leq 2N$$

Transverse derivatives reduce the actual polynomial degree of individual
terms. The value $2N$ therefore represents a conservative upper bound for
the complete total-degree basis.

For a tensor-product Maclaurin basis, where each function may contain powers
up to $N$ independently in both transverse coordinates, the total degree of
a single basis function may reach $2N$ and the product of two functions may
reach

$$p_T \leq 4N$$

Therefore,

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} \leq 4N p_g$$

for the tensor-product basis.

The important point is that the transverse CUF order does not directly
represent a longitudinal degree. It becomes a longitudinal contribution only
through its composition with the varying transverse geometry.


## 5. Independent variation of the cross-sectional measure

The second geometric contribution is produced by the differential
cross-sectional measure.

Using the reference domain $\widehat{\Omega}$,

$$d\Omega = J_\Omega(x,\eta,\zeta) \, d\eta \, d\zeta$$

where $J_\Omega$ is the Jacobian determinant of the transverse geometric
mapping.

The sectional integral can therefore be written as

$$J_{\tau s}^{mn}(x) =
\sum_k
\int_{\widehat{\Omega}_k}
C_k^{mn}
D_\phi F_\tau
D_\xi F_s
J_\Omega
\, d\eta \, d\zeta$$

The factor $J_\Omega$ produces a longitudinal polynomial contribution that is
independent of the longitudinal transfer of the transverse CUF polynomial.

If each transverse coordinate of the mapping has polynomial degree $p_g$ in
$x$, the two-dimensional Jacobian determinant can conservatively reach degree

$$p_J \leq 2p_g$$

For affine variation,

$$p_g = 1$$

and therefore

$$p_J \leq 2$$

This is the origin of the geometric contribution previously identified as

$$p_{\Omega} = 2$$

for a general polygonal section with affine vertex variation.

The two geometric effects must not be confused:

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} = p_g p_T$$

describes the longitudinal variation acquired by the transverse polynomial,
whereas

$$p_J \leq 2p_g$$

describes the independent longitudinal variation of the cross-sectional
measure.

Because these factors appear multiplicatively in the sectional integral,
their polynomial degrees are additive.


## 6. Material variation

The constitutive field may also vary along the beam axis:

$$C_{\mathrm{CSF}} = C_{\mathrm{CSF}}(x,y,z)$$

Let $p_C$ denote the polynomial degree of its longitudinal variation.

If the constitutive term enters multiplicatively in the sectional
coefficient, its degree must also contribute to the longitudinal estimate.

For the current linear material-variation cases,

$$p_C = 1$$

A constant constitutive field has

$$p_C = 0$$

For a user-defined constitutive law, $p_C$ cannot in general be inferred by
CUF.

The material provider, or the user defining the custom law, must therefore
supply the corresponding longitudinal polynomial degree.

For example, a cubic constitutive law requires

$$p_C = 3$$

If the custom law is not polynomial, a finite polynomial exactness degree
cannot in general be assigned and the Gauss-order criterion must be treated
accordingly.


## 7. Longitudinal finite-element contribution

The sectional coefficient is subsequently multiplied by the longitudinal
finite-element functions.

A generic term entering the element matrix has the structure

$$D^r N_a(x) \, J_{\tau s}^{mn}(x) \, D^q N_b(x)$$

Let $p_{\mathrm{FE}}$ denote the polynomial degree of the product

$$D^r N_a(x) \, D^q N_b(x)$$

For two undifferentiated longitudinal interpolation functions of polynomial
order $R$, a conservative upper bound is

$$p_{\mathrm{FE}} = 2R$$

Longitudinal derivatives reduce the degree of the corresponding individual
terms.


## 8. Complete polynomial-degree propagation

For one multiplicative term of the CUF fundamental nucleus, the conservative
longitudinal polynomial degree is therefore

$$p_{\mathrm{term}} =
p_{T\rightarrow x}
+
p_J
+
p_C
+
p_{\mathrm{FE}}$$

Using the geometric-transfer relation,

$$p_{\mathrm{term}} =
p_g p_T
+
p_J
+
p_C
+
p_{\mathrm{FE}}$$

For a general affine CSF polygonal variation,

$$p_g = 1$$

and

$$p_J \leq 2$$

so that

$$p_{\mathrm{term}} \leq
p_T + 2 + p_C + p_{\mathrm{FE}}$$

For the complete total-degree Maclaurin basis, using the conservative bound

$$p_T \leq 2N$$

this becomes

$$p_{\mathrm{term}} \leq
2N + 2 + p_C + p_{\mathrm{FE}}$$

For the tensor-product basis,

$$p_T \leq 4N$$

and therefore

$$p_{\mathrm{term}} \leq
4N + 2 + p_C + p_{\mathrm{FE}}$$

for affine transverse geometry.

The CUF fundamental nucleus contains several such terms. These terms are
added, not multiplied.

Therefore the degree required for the complete longitudinal integration is
the maximum degree among the individual terms:

$$p_{\mathrm{long}} =
\max_{\mathrm{terms}}
\left(
p_{\mathrm{term}}
\right)$$

The polynomial degrees of different additive nucleus terms must not be summed
with each other.


## 9. Algebraic propagation rule

The degree estimate must follow the actual algebraic operations appearing in
the integrand.

For polynomial functions $f$ and $g$,

$$\deg(fg) = \deg(f) + \deg(g)$$

whereas

$$\deg(f+g) \leq \max(\deg(f),\deg(g))$$

and

$$\deg(f^k) = k \deg(f)$$

unless cancellation of the highest-order terms occurs.

Therefore the quadrature degree must be derived from the complete
mathematical expression rather than by assigning independent empirical
corrections to its components.


## 10. Gauss-Legendre requirement

An $n$-point Gauss-Legendre rule integrates exactly a polynomial of degree up
to

$$2n-1$$

Therefore the minimum longitudinal quadrature order is

$$n_G =
\left\lceil
\frac{p_{\mathrm{long}}+1}{2}
\right\rceil$$

All geometric, transverse-expansion, constitutive and longitudinal
finite-element contributions must be included in $p_{\mathrm{long}}$ before
this relation is applied.


## 11. Constant-section limit

A constant cross-section is the degenerate limit of the general CSF
description.

In this case,

$$p_g = 0$$

and

$$p_J = 0$$

Consequently, the transverse CUF polynomial does not acquire any additional
longitudinal dependence:

$$p_{T\rightarrow x} = 0$$

The longitudinal quadrature degree is then determined only by the
longitudinal finite-element interpolation, the constitutive variation, and
any other explicitly longitudinal quantities.

The implementation may nevertheless use conservative upper bounds associated
with the general variable-section case when it is desirable for CUF to remain
agnostic about whether the supplied CSF section is prismatic or variable.

Such a choice produces over-integration in the constant-section limit but
prevents under-integration when the same formulation is applied to a
continuously varying cross-section.


## 12. Interpretation for the CSF-CUF extension

The principal consequence of the CSF-CUF variable-section extension is that
transverse and longitudinal polynomial orders are no longer independent.

For a prismatic section, a transverse polynomial remains purely transverse.

For a variable section, the same polynomial is evaluated on a geometry that
changes along the beam axis and therefore acquires a longitudinal polynomial
dependence.

At the same time, the cross-sectional measure itself varies along the beam
axis.

The longitudinal quadrature requirement must therefore account for both

$$\text{transverse polynomial} \rightarrow \text{longitudinal dependence}$$

and

$$\text{cross-sectional measure} \rightarrow \text{additional longitudinal dependence}$$

together with material and longitudinal finite-element variations.

This coupling is absent from the classical constant-section case and is an
intrinsic part of the extension of CUF to a general continuous CSF
cross-sectional representation.
